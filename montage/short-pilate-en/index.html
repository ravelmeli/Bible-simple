In 1961,

archaeologists turn over a

stone and read the

name of Pontius Pilate.

We are at Caesarea,

on the Mediterranean coast,

in a ruined Roman

theater.

An Italian team spots

a block of limestone,

reused as a simple

stair step.

On one face,

a Latin inscription,

damaged but readable:

"Pontius Pilatus," then "prefect

of Judea." The first

line is incomplete.

Many read "Tiberieum," a

building dedicated to the

emperor Tiberius,

but that reading is

still debated.

Pilate governs Judea from

about AD 26 to

36,

under Tiberius.

Luke places him exactly

there,

in the World English

Bible:

of Judea." Luke writes

"governor." The stone gives

his official title:

prefect.

"Governor" is a more

general word.

Pilate wasn't unknown:

the Jewish historian Josephus,

the philosopher Philo and

the Roman historian Tacitus

all mention him.

But this stone is

the most famous engraved

trace of his time.

What it shows:

a Roman prefect named

Pontius Pilate really existed,

in the place and

time the Gospels give.

What it doesn't show:

the trial of Jesus.

That is a matter

of texts,

not of stone.

The original is in

the Israel Museum in

Jerusalem.

A copy stands at

Caesarea,

where it was found.

the Gospels would you

like to see found

on a stone?
