One evening, a whole city comes knocking at a single door.
It is Capernaum, on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. The sun has just set, which ends the Sabbath.
Mark writes, in the World English Bible: "At evening, when the sun had set, they brought to him all who were sick, and those who were possessed by demons. All the city was gathered together at the door."
This scene sums up the Gospels: Jesus heals, and he heals many.
But how many healings do the Gospels really tell, one by one?
Depending on how you count, about twenty individual accounts, plus many summaries like this one. The text itself gives no total.
In this study, we will go through them, sorted by type of illness: the skin, the eyes, the ears and speech, movement, fever and blood, spirits, and death.
At each step, we will look at what the text says, and what Jesus does exactly. Because the gestures change from one account to another.
A word on method: we keep the accounts where a person, or a small group, is healed with their own story. Crowds healed all at once are summaries.
Let's begin with the skin, and the most feared disease of the time: leprosy.
The Greek word is lepra. But in the Bible, this term and its Hebrew equivalent cover a range of skin conditions, described in chapter thirteen of Leviticus. Most specialists think it is not necessarily the disease we call leprosy today.
The sick person had to live apart and cry out: "Unclean!" It was an exclusion as much as an illness.
A man with leprosy comes to Jesus, in Mark, chapter one, and says to him: "If you want to, you can make me clean."
Jesus, says the text, is "moved with compassion." Some ancient manuscripts say "angry"; specialists debate it. In every case, he stretches out his hand and touches him.
Touching a leper made you unclean. Jesus does it before he even speaks. Then he says: "I want to. Be made clean." And he sends him to show himself to the priest, as the law required.
Later, in Luke, chapter seventeen, ten lepers call out to him from a distance. Jesus does not touch them: he sends them to the priests, and they are cleansed on the way.
Only one comes back to say thank you, and the text specifies: he was a Samaritan, a foreigner.
Take your time. If you want to go through the rest with us, it is in the same vein.
Second category: sight. It is one of the most frequent in the Gospels.
At Jericho, a blind beggar, Bartimaeus, cries out: "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" The crowd tells him to be quiet. He cries out louder.
When Jesus calls him, he throws off his cloak, jumps up, and comes. To the question "What do you want me to do for you?", his answer is one word: to see.
Jesus tells him: "Go your way. Your faith has made you well." In Greek, the verb is sozo, which means both to save and to heal. Other translations say "saved." The text often plays on both meanings, and it is not always possible to separate them.
At Bethsaida, Mark tells of a healing in two stages, the only one of its kind. After a first gesture, the man says: "I see men; for I see them like trees walking." Jesus lays his hands on him again, and the man sees everything clearly.
The text does not explain it. It simply shows that a healing can happen in stages.
And then there is the man born blind, in John, chapter nine. The disciples ask: who sinned, this man or his parents? Jesus answers that it was neither.
He makes mud with his saliva, puts it on the man's eyes, and tells him to go and wash in the pool of Siloam, a name that means "sent." The man goes, and comes back seeing.
The neighbors hesitate, the authorities question him, his parents stay silent out of fear. He repeats one simple thing: "though I was blind, now I see."
Third category: hearing and speech.
In the Decapolis, a deaf man who can hardly speak is brought to Jesus. Jesus takes him aside, away from the crowd. He puts his fingers in his ears, touches his tongue with saliva, looks up to heaven, and sighs.
Then he says an Aramaic word that Mark kept as it was: Ephphatha, which means "Be opened."
The crowd cries out: "He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak."
A mute man is also brought to Jesus in Matthew, chapter nine. His silence is blamed on an evil spirit, and he speaks as soon as the spirit is driven out.
Fourth category: movement. The paralyzed, hands and backs.
At Capernaum, four men carry their paralyzed friend. The house is full; they open the roof, and lower him in front of Jesus.
Jesus sees their faith, and first says: "Son, your sins are forgiven you." Some scribes are outraged. Then he orders the man to get up, take his mat, and go home.
In Jerusalem, near the pool of Bethesda, John tells the story of a man who has been sick for thirty-eight years. Jesus asks him a surprising question: "Do you want to be made well?"
Around him, a crowd of sick people. The text tells only this one healing. So the Gospels do not tell everything, and in this account, Jesus does not heal every sick person present.
On a Sabbath, in a synagogue, a man has a withered hand. Jesus says to him: "Stretch out your hand." The hand is restored, and the debate about the Sabbath begins.
In Luke, chapter thirteen, a woman has been bent over for eighteen years. Jesus calls her, lays his hands on her, and names her "a daughter of Abraham." She stands up straight, and glorifies God.
Fifth category: fever and blood.
Peter's mother-in-law is in bed with a fever. Jesus comes close, takes her by the hand, and lifts her up. The fever leaves her, and she begins to serve them.
At Capernaum again, a royal official begs him for his dying son. Jesus simply says: "Go your way. Your son lives." The man believes him, leaves, and learns on the way that the fever left the child at the very hour of that word.
A Roman centurion asks for the healing of his servant, and says that Jesus does not need to come to his house: a word is enough. Jesus is amazed at this faith, and the servant is healed from a distance.
Then there is a woman who has been bleeding for twelve years. She has suffered much under many doctors, has spent everything she had, and has found no relief.
She says to herself: if I just touch his clothes, I will be healed. She touches, and the bleeding stops. Jesus asks: "Who touched my clothes?" She comes, trembling, and tells him the whole truth.
He calls her "daughter," and says: "Your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease."
Sixth category: spirits. The Gospels often distinguish illness from the casting out of an unclean spirit, even if the two are sometimes linked.
Specialists have long discussed how these accounts relate to what we call mental or neurological illnesses today. The text itself makes no medical diagnosis, and we will not make one either.
In the synagogue at Capernaum, a man cries out: "I know who you are: the Holy One of God!" Jesus orders the spirit to be silent and to come out of the man. The crowd is astonished: he speaks with authority.
In the country of the Gadarenes, a man lives among the tombs, and no one can chain him any more. After his healing, he is found sitting, clothed, and in his right mind.
A father brings his son, who has violent seizures. Jesus tells him: "All things are possible to him who believes." And the father answers: "I believe. Help my unbelief!"
A Canaanite mother, a foreigner, pleads for her daughter. Jesus seems to hesitate at first, then says: "Woman, great is your faith!" Her daughter is healed from a distance.
Last category: death. The Gospels tell of three cases where Jesus gives life back.
Jairus's daughter, twelve years old, whose death is announced. Jesus takes her by the hand and says, in Aramaic: Talitha koumi, "Girl, get up." She gets up and walks, and he asks that she be given something to eat.
At Nain, he meets a funeral procession: the only son of a widow. The text says he is moved with compassion for her, and he says to the young man: "Arise."
Finally, Lazarus, dead for four days. Jesus weeps in front of the tomb, then cries out: "Lazarus, come out!"
Christian tradition sees in them signs that point to the resurrection of Jesus. According to the usual reading, these people later died again.
Now, let's look at the whole. What do these accounts have in common, and how do they differ?
The gestures vary: a word, a touch, mud, saliva, a healing from a distance. There is no single formula.
Faith appears often, but not always: the faith of the sick person, of their friends, of a father or a centurion. At Nain, and for Lazarus, nothing is said about the faith of the dead person, and the man born blind asks for nothing.
And the Gospels also say that Jesus does not always heal. At Nazareth, Mark writes that "he could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them."
When John the Baptist, in prison, sends to ask whether Jesus is the one who is to come, Jesus answers: "the blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them."
Notice: these are almost exactly our categories. Specialists see in them an echo of the promises of Isaiah, in chapters thirty-five and sixty-one.
In other words, these healings are not only relief. In John, they are called signs: they announce that something new is beginning.
But these accounts do not say that every believer will be healed. The New Testament also speaks of sick people who are not healed. Paul asks three times for a thorn in the flesh, whose nature the text does not tell, to be taken away, and he receives this answer: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."
In another letter, he writes that he left his co-worker Trophimus sick at Miletus.
If you pray for a healing that does not come, these texts do not say that you lack faith. Nor do they say that your suffering does not matter.
And if you are sick, these accounts do not replace treatment: seeing a doctor does not oppose faith. Paul calls Luke "the beloved physician," and tradition identifies him as the author of one of the Gospels.
One question, if you want to answer it: among these accounts, which one touches you the most, and why? Tell us in the comments.
Let's go back to the door at Capernaum. Mark writes: "He healed many who were sick with various diseases."
The Gospels do not sort sufferings; they look at them. The skin, the eyes, the voice, the legs, the blood, death: in front of each one, Jesus stops.
Perhaps that is the red thread of these accounts: no one is too far, too unclean, too sick, or too dead for him to stop.
