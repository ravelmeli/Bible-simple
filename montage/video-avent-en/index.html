The word "Advent" does

not appear anywhere in

the Bible.

And yet the whole

Bible seems to lead

to it.

On Sunday,

millions of people will

light a first candle,

open a first door

on a calendar,

and begin to wait

for Christmas.

But where does this

habit come from?

What does the Bible

really say about waiting?

And what have the

centuries added?

I will guide you

step by step,

separating what the text

says,

what tradition says,

and what is only

a modern habit.

And you will see

that Advent speaks as

much about the future

as about the past.

Let's start with the

word itself.

"Advent" comes from the

Latin adventus.

It means "arrival," "coming."

The Romans used this

word for a very

specific scene:

the solemn entry of

an emperor into a

city.

The people came out

to welcome him,

the city was decorated,

the festivities began.

The first Christians,

who spoke Greek,

had a similar word:

parousia.

It means "presence," "coming."

It too described the

visit of a king.

So when the New

Testament speaks of the

coming of Christ,

it borrows the vocabulary

of a sovereign's arrival.

The image is powerful:

facing the emperor of

Rome,

we wait for another

king.

Another Greek word returns:

epiphaneia,

"appearing." It shows up

in Paul's letters to

speak of the manifestation

of Christ.

Remember this:

Advent is not first

of all a countdown

to presents.

It is the waiting

for the arrival of

a king.

Now,

a first surprise:

the Bible mentions no

four-week period before Christmas.

This period is a

tradition of the Church,

built little by little.

In the fourth century,

in Spain,

a council already mentions

a time of preparation

before Epiphany.

In the fifth century,

in Gaul,

people fast for forty

days before Christmas.

In Rome,

it is around the

sixth century that the

four weeks are fixed.

The Eastern Churches,

for their part,

have other customs.

Among the Orthodox,

for example,

the Nativity fast begins

on November 15 and

lasts forty days.

Protestant and evangelical churches

do not all practice

Advent in the same

way:

some celebrate it carefully,

others hardly at all.

Another surprise:

the date of December

25 is not in

the Bible either.

The Gospels do not

give the date of

Jesus' birth.

The oldest trace of

a Christmas feast on

December 25 is found

in Rome,

in the fourth century.

Why this date?

Two main hypotheses are

discussed.

Some think the Church

chose a date close

to pagan sun festivals.

Others think the date

was calculated from the

date of Jesus' death.

Historians do not agree,

and no one can

settle it with certainty.

Here is the first

revelation of this video:

Advent is an ancient

and beautiful tradition,

but not a biblical

prescription.

If this path is

helping you,

stay with me a

little longer:

we still have to

see what the Bible

really says about waiting,

then where the wreath

and the calendar come

from.

So,

what does the Bible

say?

It does not speak

of Advent,

but it speaks a

great deal about waiting.

For centuries,

the people of Israel

waited for a king,

a savior,

a deliverer.

In Isaiah 9,

in the World English

Bible,

we read:

"For a child is

born to us.

A son is given

to us." In Micah

5,

the prophet announces that

the one who will

rule over Israel will

come out of Bethlehem,

a small town,

small among the clans

of Judah.

And in Isaiah 7,

a sign is given:

"Behold,

the virgin will conceive,

and bear a son,

and shall call his

name Immanuel." That last

verse deserves a pause.

The Hebrew word is

almah.

It describes a young

woman of marriageable age,

without specifying whether she

is a virgin.

The ancient Greek translation,

the Septuagint,

chose parthenos,

which can mean "virgin."

That is the word

Matthew 1 picks up,

in verse 23.

This difference of words

has been studied for

centuries.

It does not change

the general meaning:

God announces that he

will be "with us."

Immanuel means exactly that.

In Isaiah 11,

another image:

"A shoot will come

out of the stump

of Jesse." Jesse is

the father of King

David.

The trunk seems cut

down,

but life grows back.

In Jeremiah 33,

God promises:

"I will perform that

good word which I

have spoken." A promise

made to a people

in ruins,

during the exile.

And in Isaiah 40,

the verse we associate

with Advent:

"Comfort,

comfort my people," says

your God.

These texts were written

in dark times.

That is what biblical

waiting is:

a promise whispered in

the middle of ruins.

Between the end of

the Old Testament and

the birth of Jesus,

about four centuries pass.

Historians call this period

the intertestamental period.

No recognized prophet,

a people under foreign

rule.

The waiting is long,

and sometimes painful.

Then the voice of

a man in the

desert:

John the Baptist.

Luke 3 presents him

by quoting Isaiah:

"Make ready the way

of the Lord.

Make his paths straight."

This sentence is the

heart of Advent:

preparing the way.

In antiquity,

roads were repaired before

a king passed by.

In other words,

Advent means preparing your

heart the way you

prepare a road.

The first chapters of

the Gospel of Luke

are full of people

who are waiting.

First there is Zechariah,

an elderly priest.

An angel announces that

he will have a

son,

John.

He doubts,

and he loses his

speech until the child

is born.

About nine months of

silence.

It is not a

spectacular punishment,

it is more like

an imposed waiting:

he must be quiet,

and watch what is

coming.

Then there is Mary,

a young woman from

Nazareth.

When the angel announces

that she will be

the mother of the

Savior,

she answers:

"Behold,

the handmaid of the

Lord; be it to

me according to your

word." Later,

she sings a song

called the Magnificat,

which begins like this:

"My soul magnifies the

Lord." Joseph receives the

announcement in a dream,

in Matthew 1.

He hesitates,

he thinks it over,

then he takes Mary

into his home.

The scene in Bethlehem

is told by Luke

with great simplicity.

A decree of Emperor

Augustus orders a census,

and Joseph goes up

to Bethlehem with Mary.

There,

says the text,

Mary wrapped him in

cloths and laid him

in a feeding trough,

because there was no

room for them in

the inn.

The Greek word,

kataluma,

describes a guest room.

Some scholars think it

was a common room

of the house,

too full,

rather than an inn

in the modern sense.

Either way,

the awaited king arrives

in a humble place,

far from palaces,

and very far from

the solemn entry of

an emperor.

Each of these people

lives a different kind

of waiting:

doubt,

trust,

silence,

decision.

And at the end

of the story,

when Jesus is presented

at the temple,

two elderly people have

been waiting for him

all their lives:

Simeon,

who was "looking for

the consolation of Israel,"

and Anna,

a prophetess who prays

night and day.

Their waiting is rewarded.

They waited their whole

lives to see a

child,

and they recognize him.

But be careful:

Advent is not only

about Bethlehem.

In Christian tradition,

Advent has always had

two meanings.

It recalls the first

coming of Christ,

as a child,

in Bethlehem.

And it points to

his second coming,

at the end of

time.

The New Testament speaks

of this second coming.

Matthew 24:

"Watch therefore,

for you don't know

in what hour your

Lord comes." And the

last book of the

Bible ends with this

sentence,

in Revelation 22:

"Yes,

I come quickly." "Amen!

Yes,

come,

Lord Jesus." There is

even an Aramaic word

that sums up this

waiting:

maranatha.

Paul uses it at

the end of his

first letter to the

Corinthians.

This word can be

understood in two ways:

"Our Lord,

come!" or "Our Lord

comes." Scholars still discuss

the best translation.

In both cases,

it is a prayer

that cries out the

waiting:

Come.

That is why Advent

is a strange season:

we look back toward

Bethlehem,

and ahead toward a

return we are still

waiting for.

Now let's talk about

what was added.

The Advent wreath,

with its four candles,

is fairly recent.

It was born in

the nineteenth century,

in Germany,

with a Lutheran pastor

named Johann Hinrich Wichern,

who cared for poor

children in Hamburg.

He lit candles each

day to help them

count the days until

Christmas.

The Advent calendar,

with its little doors,

also comes from Germany,

at the same period.

As for the four

candles,

many churches give them

names:

hope,

peace,

joy,

love.

But these names change

from one tradition to

another,

and no biblical text

fixes them.

These are beautiful and

useful gestures,

but gestures of the

Church,

not commandments of the

Bible.

So what can we

keep from Advent today?

First,

the waiting.

In a world where

everything is immediate,

learning to wait is

almost an act of

resistance.

Then,

the preparation.

"Make ready the way"

is not a shopping

list:

it is an invitation

to slow down.

And finally,

hope.

In the Bible,

to hope is not

to cherish illusions,

it is to trust

that God keeps his

promises,

even when the time

is long.

In Hebrew,

one word comes back

often:

tiqvah,

usually translated "hope." It

comes from a verb,

qavah,

which means "to wait,"

"to stretch toward." The

same word,

tiqvah,

also means a cord.

In Joshua 2,

the prostitute Rahab is

told to tie a

scarlet cord in her

window:

that cord is a

tiqvah.

Many readers see in

it the idea of

a stretched cord,

of a waiting that

holds.

This is a common

explanation,

but it remains a

reading:

the text does not

state it itself.

And in the New

Testament,

the third week of

Advent bears a Latin

name,

Gaudete,

which means "rejoice." It

comes from Philippians 4:

"Rejoice in the Lord

always!" Even in the

middle of waiting,

the Bible invites us

to joy.

It is not a

joy that denies weariness:

it is a joy

that knows the road

is moving forward.

I also want to

say a word to

those for whom this

season is hard.

For many,

the end of the

year awakens loneliness,

grief,

exhaustion,

the feeling of being

left out of the

celebrations.

If that is you,

you are not alone.

The Bible does not

hide these moments:

Hannah,

the future mother of

Samuel,

waited for a child

for years,

and she wept.

And if this sadness

becomes heavy,

talking to a loved

one,

to a pastor,

or to a health

professional can really help.

It is not a

lack of faith.

Let's take a moment

to ask ourselves this

question:

what are you really

waiting for this year,

and what do these

texts change for you?

Tell me in the

comments,

I read your answers.

Back to our starting

question.

Advent is not written

in the Bible,

and yet the whole

text speaks of waiting.

The word comes from

the language of emperors,

and it was taken

up to say that

another king is coming.

Tradition added weeks,

candles,

doors to open.

It is beautiful,

and it is not

required.

What the Bible brings

is a simpler message:

prepare your road,

stay watchful,

and hope.

On Sunday,

when you light the

first candle,

you will know what

it means,

and what it does

not mean.
