The end of the world has already been announced, and postponed, many times.
On May twenty-first, two thousand eleven, an American Christian radio host, Harold Camping, says that everything will stop that day.
On May twenty-first, nothing happens. So he corrects himself: it will be October twenty-first.
On October twenty-first, nothing again.
Today, it is the prophecies for two thousand twenty-seven and the quatrains of Nostradamus that are going around.
Many believers then ask themselves the same question, sometimes with anxiety: does the Bible say when it will all end?
In this study, we will look at what the text says, what history says, and what Jesus answered to exactly this question.
Let's begin with Nostradamus, because his name always comes back.
Michel de Nostredame was a French physician and astrologer, born in fifteen oh-three, who died in fifteen sixty-six.
In fifteen fifty-five, he published in Lyon The Prophecies: hundreds of quatrains, little poems of four lines, deliberately obscure.
Historians make a simple remark: these verses are so vague that almost any event can be recognized in them, but only after the fact.
The famous quatrain about "the year nineteen ninety-nine, seven months" announced the coming of a great king of terror. Nothing happened. His admirers then reinterpreted the text.
And several famous verses attributed to Nostradamus are not his. A fake quatrain about the attacks of two thousand and one was written in the nineteen nineties by a student, who wanted to show how easily a vague text can be made to say anything.
If you like moving forward patiently, one step after another, stay with us a little longer: what follows sheds light on a lot.
The Bible, for its part, judges a prophecy in a very different way.
In Deuteronomy, chapter eighteen, God gives a test. Here is what the text says, in the World English Bible: "If the thing doesn't follow, nor come to pass, that is the thing which Yahweh has not spoken."
The criterion is demanding: a precise prediction that does not come true does not come from God.
But a biblical prophet does not announce the future to impress. He calls people to return to God.
When Jonah announces: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh will be overthrown," the city repents, and the destruction does not happen. The purpose of the announcement was conversion, not a calendar.
Now, the central question: what does Jesus say about the date of the end?
The disciples ask him in chapter twenty-four of Matthew: "Tell us, when will these things be? What is the sign of your coming, and of the end of the age?"
In Greek, "end of the age" is synteleia tou aionos: literally the completion of an age, an era. The idea is not first of all a planet exploding, but a period of history coming to its close.
We also need to remember the context. Just before, Jesus has announced that the temple in Jerusalem will be destroyed, stone by stone. It happened in the year seventy, when the Romans took the city. That is a historical fact.
Christians do not all agree on which part of the discourse speaks of that event and which part speaks of the end of time. Both readings have existed for centuries.
But on one point, Jesus is remarkably clear: "But no one knows of that day and hour, not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father alone."
The Gospel of Mark reports the same saying. Christians have long discussed what it means for Jesus himself, but the text is plain: the day remains the Father's secret.
Before going up to heaven, he repeats it to his disciples, in the book of Acts, chapter one: "It isn't for you to know times or seasons which the Father has set within his own authority."
So, when someone announces a precise date for the end, they directly contradict this saying. You don't need to be a specialist: you only need to reread Matthew.
Yet Jesus does speak of signs. But look at how he begins.
"Be careful that no one leads you astray. For many will come in my name, saying, 'I am the Christ,' and will lead many astray. You will hear of wars and rumors of wars. See that you aren't troubled, for all this must happen, but the end is not yet."
His first answer is not a calendar. It is a warning against deception, and against panic.
Wars, famines, earthquakes: Jesus says these things happen, but that "the end is not yet." Turning them into a countdown is doing the opposite of what he asks.
Paul's letters show the same reflex. In Thessalonica, Christians had received messages claiming that the day of the Lord had already come, and Paul asks them not to be shaken.
In his first letter, he writes: "the day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night." A thief gives no warning.
And the apostle Peter answers those who mock the waiting: "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day. The Lord is not slow concerning his promise, as some count slowness; but is patient with us, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance."
This verse is not for calculating. It says that God's time is not measured like ours, and that the delay is patience, not slowness.
The history of announced dates is long. In the nineteenth century, an American farmer, William Miller, studies the prophecies of Daniel and concludes that Jesus will return around eighteen forty-three.
His followers finally set a date: October twenty-second, eighteen forty-four. Tens of thousands of people wait for it. Some had left their fields or sold their possessions.
Nothing happens. This episode has a name: the Great Disappointment.
Several communities that came out of this movement, including the Seventh-day Adventist churches, later gave up setting dates.
We also hear that all of Europe panicked as the year one thousand approached. Historians now think that this general panic is largely a legend built later.
For twenty twelve, the end of the world was announced because of the Maya calendar. Specialists of the Maya explain that it was the end of a great counting cycle, not a prediction of the end of the world.
The pattern repeats: a date, a fear, a silence, then a new date.
Why do these announcements attract so many people? We might think that uncertainty is frightening, and that a date gives the impression of taking back control.
The Bible answers fear differently: not with a date, but with a way of living.
There remains the book most quoted in these discussions: the Apocalypse.
Its name comes from the Greek apokalypsis, which means unveiling, revelation. The word does not mean catastrophe.
From the start, the book says: "Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of the prophecy, and keep the things that are written in it, for the time is at hand."
The book is addressed to seven very real churches in Asia Minor, in present-day Turkey, living under the Roman Empire. It speaks to them first.
Christians have read the Apocalypse in several ways for centuries. Some see mainly the world of its first readers, others the history of the Church century after century, others events still to come, and others a symbolic picture of the struggle between good and evil. None of these readings is the only possible one.
The book is written in images. Numbers, like a thousand years or a hundred and forty-four thousand, are first of all symbolic for most specialists, and nothing in the text presents them as a code to decipher.
It ends with a promise from Jesus: "Yes, I come quickly." And with the answer of believers: "Come, Lord Jesus."
In the New Testament, the coming of Christ is called parousia, a Greek word meaning presence, arrival. It was also used for the official visit of a king to a city.
The image, then, is not of a countdown, but of an expected arrival.
Jesus draws an instruction from it: "Watch therefore, for you don't know in what hour your Lord comes." The Greek verb means to stay awake, alert.
And he does not ask us to scan the sky. He tells of servants who faithfully do their work, of young women who prepare their oil, of people who feed the hungry. To watch is to faithfully do what we have been given to do.
When you hear a prophecy about the end of the world, you can ask yourself three simple questions.
Does it give a date? Then it contradicts Matthew twenty-four, verse thirty-six.
Does it push you toward fear rather than vigilance? Jesus said: "See that you aren't troubled."
Does it ask you to leave everything, or to give money? Remember his first warning: "Be careful that no one leads you astray."
If these announcements really frighten you, if anxiety takes up too much room, you don't have to carry this alone: a doctor, a therapist or a trusted friend can help. That takes nothing away from faith.

On the morning of October twenty-first, two thousand eleven, the world was still there, and the Bible had not changed: the day and the hour remain the Father's secret.
What does the Bible say about the end of the world? That it belongs to God, that it cannot be calculated, and that Christian hope depends on no date.
What the text leaves us is not a calendar, but a way of living: to watch, to love, and to hope.
