The end of the

world has already been

announced,

and postponed,

many times.

On May twenty-first,

two thousand eleven,

an American Christian radio

host,

Harold Camping,

says that everything will

stop that day.

On May twenty-first,

nothing happens.

So he corrects himself.

It will be October

twenty-first.

On October twenty-first,

nothing again.

Today,

it is the prophecies

for two thousand twenty-seven

and the quatrains of

Nostradamus that are going

around.

Many believers then ask

themselves the same question,

sometimes with anxiety.

Does the Bible say

when it will all

end?

In this study,

we will look at

what the text says,

what history says,

and what Jesus answered

to exactly this question.

Let's begin with Nostradamus,

because his name always

comes back.

Michel de Nostredame was

a French physician and

astrologer,

born in fifteen oh-three,

who died in fifteen

sixty-six.

In fifteen fifty-five,

he published in Lyon

the prophecies.

Hundreds of quatrains,

little poems of four

lines,

deliberately obscure.

Historians make a simple

remark.

These verses are so

vague that almost any

event can be recognized

in them,

but only after the

fact.

The famous quatrain about

the year nineteen ninety-nine,

seven months,

announced the coming of

a great king of

terror.

Nothing happened.

His admirers then reinterpreted

the text.

And several famous verses

attributed to Nostradamus are

not his.

A fake quatrain about

the attacks of two

thousand and one was

written in the nineteen

nineties by a student,

who wanted to show

how easily a vague

text can be made

to say anything.

If you like moving

forward patiently,

one step after another,

stay with us a

little longer.

What follows sheds light

on a lot.

The Bible,

for its part,

judges a prophecy in

a very different way.

In Deuteronomy chapter eighteen,

God gives a test.

Here is what the

text says in the

World English Bible.

The criterion is demanding.

A precise prediction that

does not come true

does not come from

God.

But a biblical prophet

does not announce the

future to impress.

He calls people to

return to God.

When Jonah announces,

yet forty days and

Nineveh will be overthrown,

the city repents and

the destruction does not

happen.

The purpose of the

announcement was conversion,

not a calendar.

Now,

the central question,

what does Jesus say

about the date of

the end?

The disciples ask him

in chapter twenty-four of

Matthew,

In Greek,

end of the age

is synteleia tou aionos,

literally,

the completion of an

age,

an era.

The idea is not,

first of all,

a planet exploding,

but a period of

history coming to its

close.

We also need to

remember the context.

Just before,

Jesus has announced that

the temple in Jerusalem

will be destroyed,

stone by stone.

It happened in the

year seventy,

when the Romans took

the city.

That is a historical

fact.

Christians do not all

agree on which part

of the discourse speaks

of that event,

and which part speaks

of the end of

time.

Both readings have existed

for centuries.

But on one point,

Jesus is remarkably clear.

The Gospel of Mark

reports the same saying.

Christians have long discussed

what it means for

Jesus himself,

but the text is

plain.

The day remains the

Father's secret.

Before going up to

heaven,

he repeats it to

his disciples,

in the book of

Acts,

chapter one.

So when someone announces

a precise date for

the end,

they directly contradict this

saying.

You don't need to

be a specialist.

You only need to

reread Matthew.

Yet,

Jesus does speak of

signs.

But look at how

he begins.

His first answer is

not a calendar.

It is a warning

against deception and against

panic.

Wars,

famines,

earthquakes.

Jesus says these things

happen,

but that the end

is not yet.

Turning them into a

countdown is doing the

opposite of what he

asks.

Paul's letters show the

same reflex.

In Thessalonica,

Christians had received messages

claiming that the day

of the Lord had

already come,

and Paul asks them

not to be shaken.

In his first letter,

he writes,

A thief gives no

warning.

And the apostle Peter

answers those who mock

the waiting.

This verse is not

for calculating.

It says that God's

time is not measured

like ours,

and that the delay

is patience,

not slowness.

The history of announced

dates is long.

In the nineteenth century,

an American farmer,

William Miller,

studies the prophecies of

Daniel and concludes that

Jesus will return around

eighteen forty-three.

His followers finally set

a date,

October twenty-second,

eighteen forty-four.

Tens of thousands of

people wait for it.

Some had left their

fields or sold their

possessions.

Nothing happens.

This episode has a

name,

the Great Disappointment.

Several communities that came

out of this movement,

including the Seventh-day Adventist

churches,

later gave up setting

dates.

We also hear that

all of Europe panicked

as the year one

thousand approached.

Historians now think that

this general panic is

largely a legend built

later.

For twenty twelve,

the end of the

world was announced because

of the Maya calendar.

Specialists of the Maya

explained that it was

the end of a

great counting cycle,

not a prediction of

the end of the

world.

The pattern repeats.

A date,

a fear,

a silence,

then a new date.

Why do these announcements

attract so many people?

We might think that

uncertainty is frightening and

that a date gives

the impression of taking

control.

The Bible answers fear

differently.

Not with a date,

but with a way

of living.

There remains the book

most quoted in these

discussions.

The Apocalypse.

Its name comes from

the Greek apokalypsis,

which means unveiling,

revelation.

The word does not

mean catastrophe.

From the start,

the book says,

The book is addressed

to seven very real

churches in Asia Minor,

in present-day Turkey,

living under the Roman

Empire.

It speaks to them

first.

Christians have read the

Apocalypse in several ways

for centuries.

Some see mainly the

world of its first

readers,

others the history of

the church,

century after century,

others events still to

come,

and others a symbolic

picture of the struggle

between good and evil.

None of these readings

is the only possible

one.

The book is written

in images.

Numbers,

like a thousand years

or a hundred and

forty-four are,

first of all,

symbolic for most specialists,

and nothing in the

text presents them as

a code to decipher.

It ends with a

promise from Jesus,

and with the answer

of believers,

come Lord Jesus.

In the New Testament,

the coming of Christ

is called parousia,

a Greek word meaning

presence,

arrival.

It was also used

for the official visit

of a king to

a city.

The image,

then,

is not of a

countdown,

but of an expected

arrival.

Jesus draws an instruction

from it.

and he does not

ask us to scan

the sky.

He tells of servants

who faithfully do their

work,

of young women who

prepare their oil,

of people who feed

the hungry.

To watch is to

faithfully do what we

have been given to

do.

When you hear a

prophecy about the end

of the world,

you can ask yourself

free,

simple questions.

Does it give a

date?

Then it contradicts Matthew

twenty-four,

verse thirty-six.

Does it push you

toward fear rather than

vigilance?

Jesus said,

see that you aren't

troubled.

Does it ask you

to leave everything or

to give money?

Remember his first warning.

Be careful that no

one leads you astray.

If these announcements really

frighten you,

if anxiety takes up

too much room,

you don't have to

carry this alone.

A doctor,

a therapist,

or a trusted friend

can help.

That takes nothing away

from faith.

Is there a promise

in the Bible that

helped you through a

time of uncertainty?

On the morning of

October twenty-first,

two thousand eleven,

the world was still

there,

and the Bible had

not changed.

The day and the

hour remain the Father's

secret.

What does the Bible

say about the end

of the world?

That it belongs to

God.

That it cannot be

calculated,

and that Christian hope

depends on no date.

What the text leaves

us is not a

calendar,

but a way of

living.

To watch,

to love,

and to hope.
