Most people who come to see Jesus do it in broad daylight, in front of everyone. This man comes at night.
His name is Nicodemus. He is rich in learning, respected, and very attached to his rank.
Yet he is about to do something surprising: knock in secret at the door of this prophet from Galilee, whom many of his colleagues despise.
Who is this man? Why does he come at night? And what happens to him next?
His story fits in three scenes, all in the Gospel of John. A visit at night, a sentence spoken in the middle of the council, then a burial.
One detail to remember: Nicodemus appears only in the Gospel of John. The three other Gospels do not mention him. It is a portrait that belongs to John alone.
And between the first scene and the last, something changes. In this study, we will see what, following the text step by step.
Let's begin by setting the scene: Jerusalem, in the first century, during the festival of Passover.
Jesus has just driven the merchants out of the temple. He is doing signs, miracles. The Gospel says that, because of these signs, many people believe in his name.
Thousands of pilgrims crowd into the city. Rumors travel fast: who is this Galilean, where does he get his authority, and what does he really want?
Among those watching, there is a man from a very different world. Here is how the Gospel presents him, in the World English Bible: "a man of the Pharisees, named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews."
The Pharisees were a very respected religious movement. They studied the law of Moses with passion and sought to live it out in everyday life.
"Ruler of the Jews" means, according to most scholars, that he belongs to the governing council, the Sanhedrin. So he is a notable, and probably a cautious man.
His name is Greek: Nicodemus means, literally, "victor over the people." The name of an important man.
And here is this man coming to find Jesus, but at night.
Why at night? The text does not say.
Some think he feared the gaze of his colleagues. Others, that he wanted a quiet conversation, away from the crowd. Others still see a symbol, because in John, night and light matter a great deal.
These are hypotheses. The Gospel simply notes: "by night." But it will repeat it later, and we will come back to it.
Here is a clue. In the Gospel of John, light and darkness keep coming back. At the very beginning, we read: "The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness hasn't overcome it."
And when Judas leaves the table to betray Jesus, John notes simply: "It was night." Night, in John, is not only an hour of the day: it is also a state of the heart.
If this path through the text pleases you, stay with us a little longer: the richest scene is still ahead.
Nicodemus speaks first: "Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs that you do, unless God is with him."
Notice the "we." He is not speaking only for himself: he speaks on behalf of others. So there are people, within the elite, who are impressed by Jesus.
And "Rabbi" is a word of respect: "my teacher." A notable calling a craftsman from Galilee "teacher."
Jesus does not thank him. He goes straight to the point: "Most certainly, I tell you, unless one is born anew, he can't see the Kingdom of God."
The Greek word is anōthen. It has two meanings: "again," but also "from above."
Nicodemus understands "again." He answers: "How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb, and be born?"
He is not a foolish man. He is a man who takes words seriously, like a good student of the law. And the word, precisely, is ambiguous.
Jesus clarifies: "Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born of water and spirit, he can't enter into the Kingdom of God."
He speaks of a birth that does not come from us: a birth received, like a gift, and not earned.
Then he adds an image: "The wind blows where it wants to, and you hear its sound, but don't know where it comes from and where it is going. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit."
The Greek word pneuma means both "wind" and "Spirit." Jesus plays on this double meaning, as we saw in another study on the Hebrew word ruach.
Nicodemus, bewildered, asks: "How can these things be?"
And Jesus answers him, with a touch of gentle irony: "Are you the teacher of Israel, and don't understand these things?"
For a teacher, this is a shock. What Nicodemus thought he had mastered slips away from him.
Then comes a sentence many people know: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life."
Jesus points back to an episode in the book of Numbers: Israelites bitten by snakes are healed by looking at a bronze serpent set on a pole.
And the text continues: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life."
Scholars discuss where exactly Jesus' words end and the evangelist's comment begins. But the general meaning stays the same.
And then? Nothing. Nicodemus no longer answers. The Gospel says neither that he believes, nor that he refuses, nor even that he leaves. The scene stops, and the night closes again.
That is what is beautiful, and a little unsettling, in this story: the conversation does not end with a conversion. It ends with a silence.
What does Nicodemus do with this silence? The text leaves us waiting. For months, perhaps years.
When we find him again, it is in chapter seven of the Gospel of John, during the festival of Tabernacles.
The temple guards have been sent to arrest Jesus. They come back empty-handed, saying: "No man ever spoke like this man!"
The Pharisee leaders are furious. They retort: "But this multitude that doesn't know the law is accursed." They despise those who listen to Jesus.
Then a man from the council speaks up. The Gospel identifies him like this: "Nicodemus (he who came to him by night, being one of them)."
He says: "Does our law judge a man, unless it first hears from him and knows what he does?"
This is not yet a declaration of faith. He does not say "Jesus is the Messiah." He defends a principle: you have no right to condemn someone without hearing him first.
But it is a courageous act. He says it in front of his colleagues, in the middle of the council, out in the open.
The answer is cutting: "Are you also from Galilee? Search, and see that no prophet has arisen out of Galilee."
He is sent back to his origins, suspected of having already crossed to the other side. The price of speaking is immediate: contempt.
Besides, his colleagues are wrong: according to the second book of Kings, the prophet Jonah came from Gath Hepher, in Galilee.
And the meeting is adjourned. No one listens to Nicodemus. But he has spoken.
First change: night has given way to day. Nicodemus is no longer only a discreet visitor. He defends a man whom others want to condemn without hearing him.
But one last episode remains, the most surprising, and the most risky.
We are now in chapter nineteen. Jesus has been crucified under Pontius Pilate. The disciples have scattered.
A man asks Pilate for the right to take down the body: Joseph of Arimathea, whom the Gospel describes as "a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews."
Joseph, then, was afraid. The text says so. For Nicodemus, the Gospel does not speak of fear.
And here is what the Gospel writes: "Nicodemus, who at first came to him by night, also came, bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds."
A hundred Roman pounds means more than seventy pounds of perfumes and spices, about thirty kilos. An enormous quantity, and very costly.
Two men, two different profiles, two possible fears. Yet they make the same gesture, together, at the worst moment.
Many readers see in it a royal funeral, almost excessive, a way of honoring Jesus as a king. This is a common interpretation, not a certainty.
Picture the scene. Jesus has just died on a Roman cross, the death reserved for the condemned. His disciples are hiding. And a member of the Sanhedrin, a rich man, a notable, comes to take care of his body.
To show yourself at that moment is to risk your reputation, perhaps your position, perhaps more.
They wrap the body in linen cloths with the spices, as the Jews are accustomed to do, and lay it in a new tomb, in a garden, very close to the place of the crucifixion.
So there it is: the man who came by night, in secret, is now here in broad daylight, when almost everyone else has gone.
Notice also the evangelist's expression: "who at first came by night." John recalls the first visit to measure the road traveled.
Yet let's be honest: the Gospel never says "Nicodemus became a disciple." It says nothing more about his life after this episode, and the book of Acts does not mention him.
Christian tradition has regarded him as a believer. Much later, several centuries afterward, an apocryphal writing called the "Gospel of Nicodemus" was published under his name. It is not part of the Bible.
What we have, then, is this: three scenes. A night, a word, a gesture.
What does this story tell us? First, that we can approach Jesus with our questions, our doubts and our fears. Nicodemus comes with a doubt, leaves with a riddle, and Jesus does not drive him away.
Next, that faith can be slow. It is not always a flash. For Nicodemus, it seems to move forward in stages: a visit, a defense, an act.
Finally, that courage often grows in small steps: a quiet conversation, then a word, then a public gesture.
This story does not judge those who move forward in the shadows. It does not condemn them, but it does not leave them where they are either.
If you carry a fear, the fear of other people's gaze, of what you risk by believing, you are not alone. If this fear becomes very heavy, with anxiety or isolation, speaking to someone you trust, or to a professional, can help.
