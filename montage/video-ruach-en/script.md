On Mount Horeb, the prophet Elijah waits.
A wind so strong it tears the rocks apart passes in front of him. Then an earthquake. Then a fire.
And three times, the text repeats it: Yahweh was not in it.
Yet the word the text uses for that wind is the very same word that means "spirit."
That word is ruach.
In English, it is translated "wind," or "breath," or "spirit." Three words for one single Hebrew word.
Our word "spirit" itself comes from the Latin spiritus, which also meant "breath." Greek does the same thing with the word pneuma.
So why? Is it a confusion, or a message?
In this study, we will follow ruach through the Bible, one passage at a time.
Let's begin with the scene at Horeb, in the first book of Kings, chapter nineteen.
Here is what the text says, in the World English Bible: "Behold, Yahweh passed by, and a great and strong wind tore the mountains, and broke in pieces the rocks before Yahweh; but Yahweh was not in the wind. After the wind, an earthquake; but Yahweh was not in the earthquake."
Here, ruach means a real wind, a phenomenon of nature. This is the first meaning of the word.
In the Bible, the wind is never far from the action of God.
When Israel is trapped in front of the sea, in the book of Exodus, the text says: "Yahweh caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all night."
It is the word ruach again. And it is also the word for the "four winds," the four directions from which the breath comes.
First meaning: the wind. A force you cannot see, but whose effects you can.
If you like moving forward this way, one word at a time, stay with us a little longer. The path gets more beautiful as we go.
Second meaning: the breath. The one coming out of your mouth right now.
In Psalm one hundred and four, the poet looks at every living creature and says to God: "You take away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. You send out your Spirit, they are created. You renew the surface of the ground."
Here, ruach is the breath of life. When it leaves, life stops. When it is sent, life begins again.
One detail worth knowing: in the story where God forms man, in Genesis chapter two, the text speaks of the "breath of life." But the Hebrew word is not ruach. It is another word, neshamah.
The two words are close, but they are not interchangeable. The Bible has several words for breath, and ruach is often tied to movement and force.
Ecclesiastes, which speaks about death, writes: "the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it."
Here the third meaning appears: the spirit.
Third meaning: the spirit. A person's inner life, and the Spirit of God.
From the second verse of the Bible, ruach is there: "the Spirit of God was hovering over the surface of the waters."
Here, let's be honest: translators do not agree. Some write "the Spirit of God," others "a wind from God" or "a mighty wind." The Hebrew word allows both readings, and the text does not settle it.
Ruach can also describe the inside of a person. When Jacob learns that his son Joseph is alive, the Bible says that "the spirit of Jacob their father revived."
And Psalm thirty-four promises: "Yahweh is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves those who have a crushed spirit."
One word: a wind, a breath, a spirit. But where do we see all three together? In a valley, in the book of Ezekiel.
Chapter thirty-seven. The prophet stands in the middle of a valley filled with dry bones. God tells him to speak to those bones.
Then he says: "Prophesy to the wind, prophesy, son of man, and say to the wind, Thus says the Lord Yahweh: Come from the four winds, breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live."
In the Hebrew, one word comes back again and again in this chapter: ruach. Wind, breath, spirit. The text plays with all three meanings at once, and several scholars point it out.
The text itself gives the key to the image: these bones are "the whole house of Israel," discouraged, in exile.
And the final promise fits in one sentence: "I will put my Spirit in you, and you will live."
The New Testament, written in Greek, keeps this play on words with pneuma, which also means wind, breath, and spirit.
At night, Jesus speaks with a man named Nicodemus. He says: "The wind blows where it wants to, and you hear its sound, but don't know where it comes from and where it is going. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit."
In the Greek, it is the same word for "wind" and for "Spirit." Jesus spoke Aramaic, and the related Aramaic word also carries these meanings. This play on words was therefore probably natural to him: that is a reasonable hypothesis, not a certainty.
After the resurrection, in the Gospel of John, Jesus "breathed on them, and said to them, Receive the Holy Spirit." A breath.
And at Pentecost, there came from the sky "a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind."
From the wind at Horeb to the wind at Pentecost, the Bible keeps this word: the invisible that acts.
But careful. Just because a word has three meanings does not mean all three fit everywhere: context decides.
The Bible does not say that God is the wind. Elijah reminds us: Yahweh was not in the wind. The wind is a sign, not God himself.
