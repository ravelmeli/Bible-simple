## ➤ Video description: 

On Mount Horeb, Elijah sees a violent wind, an earthquake and a fire, and the text repeats: Yahweh was not in it. Yet the Hebrew word for that wind is ruach, the very same word that means "spirit" and "breath." Why one word for three meanings? In this study, we follow ruach through the Bible, one passage at a time.

We first discover the first meaning, the wind (1 Kings 19 and Exodus 14), then the second, the breath of life (Psalm 104 and Ecclesiastes 12), with an important detail: in Genesis 2:7, the "breath of life" is a different word, neshamah. The third meaning is the spirit, a person's inner life and the Spirit of God, from Genesis 1:2. We say honestly that translators do not agree on that verse: "the Spirit of God" or "a wind from God."

The high point of the study is Ezekiel 37, the valley of dry bones, where the word ruach comes back again and again and plays with all three meanings at once. We then see how the New Testament, in Greek, keeps this play on words with pneuma: Jesus and Nicodemus (John 3:8), the breath of the risen Jesus (John 20:22) and the wind of Pentecost (Acts 2:2). That Jesus played on this double meaning in Aramaic is a reasonable hypothesis, not a certainty.

Finally, we remind ourselves that context decides: a word having three meanings does not mean all three fit everywhere, and the Bible does not say that God is the wind. A simple practice for this week, with a sentence on the value of support when the spirit is crushed.
