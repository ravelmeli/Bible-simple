"Hallelujah" is not a cry of joy. It is a command.
In Hebrew, the word has two parts: Hallelu, and Yah.
Hallelu comes from the verb halal, "to praise." It is an imperative, in the plural: "Praise!"
So it isn't addressed to one person alone, but to a whole group: "Praise, all of you!"
And Yah is a short form of God's name, the one written YHWH.
So hallelujah means: "Praise Yah!"
You find it mostly in the Psalms. The last one ends like this, in the World English Bible: "Let everything that has breath praise Yah! Praise Yah!"
In the Hebrew text, that last sentence is: Hallelu-Yah.
The word travelled: Greek wrote it without translating it, then Latin, then our languages.
In the New Testament, it appears only in Revelation, in chapter nineteen, in the mouth of a great crowd.
This isn't against joy: praising is often singing. But the word itself is first of all a call, to praise together.
Next time you hear "hallelujah," remember: you are being spoken to.
