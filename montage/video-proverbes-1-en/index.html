Somewhere in a city

of ancient Israel,

a woman is shouting

in the middle of

the marketplace.

Nobody paid her to

do it.

She has no stall,

no army,

no title.

Yet she raises her

voice at the city

gates,

right where the decisions

are made.

This woman is wisdom,

and she appears in

the very first chapter

of the book of

Proverbs.

Here is a question

many people ask when

they open the Bible.

Where do I even

begin to understand it

all?

There are reading plans,

summaries,

and commentaries,

and still,

you sometimes feel that

one key is missing.

In this study,

we'll see why Proverbs

1 can play that

role,

not as the only

key,

but as a key

to reading that the

Bible itself gives us.

Let's start with the

context.

The book opens like

this,

in the World English

Bible.

The Proverbs of Solomon,

Solomon reigns around the

tenth century before our

era.

Tradition presents him as

the wisest king of

his time.

Historians think the book

took its present shape

later.

The text itself hints

at it.

In chapter twenty-five,

it says that men

of King Hezekiah copied

out more of Solomon's

Proverbs.

Several scholars also point

to similarities with an

Egyptian wisdom text,

the Instruction of Amenemope,

a sign that wisdom

was a shared language

in the ancient world.

If you enjoy going

step by step,

one passage at a

time,

stay with us a

little longer.

This road is easier

together.

But what does this

book want to do?

It tells us right

away in its first

verses.

Here is what it

announces.

To know wisdom and

Let's pause on three

Hebrew words.

The first is chokhmah,

which we translate as

wisdom.

In the Hebrew Bible,

this word is not

first about theory.

It is also used

for the skill of

a craftsman.

In Exodus,

Bezalel,

who builds the sanctuary,

is given wisdom for

his work.

Wisdom is knowing how

to make and knowing

how to live.

The second word is

musar,

instruction or discipline.

It is the kind

of teaching that instructs

as much as it

corrects.

The third is found

in verse seven,

yirah,

fear.

Here is the verse.

The fear of Yahweh

The word fear can

be surprising.

In the Old Testament,

it doesn't only describe

dread.

read.

It also covers respect,

wonder,

an awareness of who

God is and of

who we are.

And this verse sets

an order.

We don't begin to

understand by placing ourselves

at the center,

but by recognizing God

in His place.

For many readers,

this is where the

key is found.

The book doesn't say,

be clever and you

will find God.

It says,

recognize God and understanding

will begin.

After this prologue,

the chapter unfolds in

two scenes.

First scene,

verses 8-19,

a father speaks to

his son.

My son,

Then he warns him.

Men will come and

invite him to follow

them.

They offer him a

share of the loot

and a common purse.

The text exposes the

irony.

These men set traps,

but the traps close

on themselves.

Here is the book's

first choice.

Two roads.

The one that promises

quick gains,

and the one that

builds slowly.

Second scene,

verses 20-33.

Wisdom herself begins to

speak.

A wisdom that calls

out in the streets.

She is not hidden

on a mountaintop,

reserved for an elite.

She is offered to

everyone,

in the middle of

the noise.

But she meets a

refusal.

She calls,

and nobody listens.

The text then presents

the consequences as the

fruit of our own

choices.

And the chapter ends

on a promise.

So why talk about

a key for the

whole Bible?

First,

because this idea runs

through other books.

In Psalm one hundred eleven,

verse ten,

the fear of Yahweh

Almost the same words.

In the book of

Job,

chapter twenty-eight,

after a long search

for wisdom,

like digging for gold

in the earth,

the answer comes.

And Ecclesiastes,

after trying everything,

concludes in his own

way.

Then the New Testament

picks up this language.

Jesus declares that someone

greater than Solomon is

here.

Paul writes that Christ

is the power of

God and the wisdom

of God.

And James writes,

but if any of

you lacks wisdom,

let him ask of

God.

Here,

let's be honest.

Reading Jesus as the

wisdom of God is

a Christian reading,

built on these texts.

Jewish readers of Proverbs

do not make it.

What Proverbs 1 says

is that wisdom begins

with a relationship with

God,

and that it calls

everyone.

What Christian tradition adds

is that this wisdom

So,

a key for the

whole Bible?

Here's what we can

honestly say.

Proverbs 1 gives a

key to reading.

It tells us how

to read,

with humility,

seeking to live what

we understand,

rather than collecting information.

This week,

try this.

Slowly reread the first

seven verses,

then ask yourself one

simple question.

At which crossroads of

my life is a

voice calling,

and what am I

doing with what I

already hear?

Is there a verse

from Proverbs that has

already guided you,

or a decision for

which you are still

seeking wisdom?

Write it in the

comments.

No pressure.

The woman in the

marketplace is still shouting.

She isn't shouting to

shame you,

but because she knows

where the two roads

lead.

She doesn't wait wait

for you to know

everything.

She only asks you

to begin in the

right place,

to recognize God in

His place.
