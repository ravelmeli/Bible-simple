Almost everyone thinks this

sentence is in the

Bible.

It isn't.

"God helps those who

help themselves." We say

it to someone going

through a trial.

We repeat it to

students before an exam.

And in many churches,

it is heard almost

like a verse.

Yet if you read

the Bible from Genesis

to Revelation,

you will find neither

this sentence,

nor even one that

really resembles it.

So where does it

come from?

Who said it first?

And above all:

is it true,

according to the Bible?

In this study,

we will follow the

sentence's trail,

from seventeenth-century France back

to ancient Greece.

Then we will see

what the biblical text

actually says about God's

help.

Let's begin with the

best-known source:

a French poet.

In 1668,

Jean de La Fontaine

publishes his first fables.

In the sixth book,

he tells the story

of a carter stuck

in a muddy road.

The wheel of his

cart has sunk into

the mud.

The poor man shouts,

he complains,

and he calls on

Hercules for help.

Hercules is a hero

of Greek mythology,

whom the ancients worshipped

as a demigod.

In the fable,

Hercules answers.

He tells the carter

to look at the

cause of his trouble:

to put his shoulder

to the wheel,

to clear the stones,

to fill the rut.

Then he ends with

the sentence we are

following,

which in French reads:

"Aide-toi,

le Ciel t'aidera." Help

yourself,

and Heaven will help

you.

Keep this detail in

mind:

the one speaking is

not the God of

Israel.

It is Hercules,

a pagan deity.

The sentence is born

in the mouth of

a Greek god.

Yet La Fontaine did

not invent the story.

He borrows it,

as he often does,

from Aesop,

a storyteller of ancient

Greece.

In Aesop's version,

a man whose wagon

is stuck prays to

Hercules to come and

help him.

Hercules answers,

in essence,

to put his hand

to the work before

praying.

So the idea is

very old:

long before Jesus,

Greeks were already telling

that the gods do

not help those who

stand with folded arms.

That is the first

stage:

the sentence comes from

a Greek fable,

taken up by a

French poet.

If this journey through

the history of words

pleases you,

come along with us

a little longer:

what follows changes how

we look at this

sentence.

For there is also

an English version,

the one you know:

"God helps those who

help themselves." We already

find it in a

collection of proverbs by

the English poet George

Herbert,

in 1651,

in the form:

"Help thyself,

and God will help

thee." The political thinker

Algernon Sidney writes it

too,

at the end of

the seventeenth century,

saying that it is

a proverb.

And it is the

American Benjamin Franklin who

makes it immensely popular,

by publishing it in

his almanac in 1736.

Franklin was not a

theologian.

He was a printer,

a practical man.

He presents it as

a proverb,

not as a verse.

The most curious part

comes next.

In the late 1820s,

in France,

a group of liberal

opponents takes the formula

as its motto:

the society "Aide-toi,

le ciel t'aidera." Over

time,

the sentence moves from

fables to politics,

from almanacs to speeches,

then to sermons.

By being repeated in

churches,

it leads many people

to believe it is

biblical.

Polls conducted in the

United States show,

moreover,

that a large share

of believers think it

is found in the

Bible.

Let's sum up:

the sentence is not

in the Bible.

It comes from a

fable,

then a popular proverb,

then centuries of practical

morality.

But a question remains:

even if it is

not in the Bible,

does it say something

true?

To answer,

let's look at what

the Bible really says

about God's help.

And the first surprise

is that the text

says almost the opposite.

Take the Hebrew word

ezer,

which means "help" or

"aid." It is used

several times to speak

of God himself.

In Psalm thirty-three,

verse twenty,

in the World English

Bible:

and our shield." God

is presented as the

help of those who

cannot get out of

trouble on their own.

In Psalm one hundred

twenty-one:

earth." And in Psalm

forty-six:

in trouble." Notice:

in these texts,

no one says "first,

help yourself." The help

comes from God,

sometimes before any effort.

The New Testament goes

even further.

Paul writes,

in the letter to

the Romans,

chapter five:

ungodly." "Weak":

that is far from

"help yourself." God's help

arrives at a moment

when we can do

nothing.

And on salvation,

Paul is even clearer,

in the letter to

the Ephesians,

chapter two:

boast." In other words:

if "God helps those

who help themselves" meant

"save yourself,

and God will finish

the job," the Bible

would say no.

Salvation is a gift.

But it would be

too simple to stop

there.

For the Bible does

not say either that

effort is useless.

Listen to the book

of Proverbs,

chapter six:

and be wise." The

text values work,

foresight,

practical wisdom.

Paul too,

in his second letter

to the Thessalonians:

Personal effort is not

forbidden.

It is even valued

as a normal part

of the life God

gives us.

But beware of the

context:

Paul is speaking of

those who refuse to

work,

not of those who

cannot.

He himself recalls that

he worked with his

own hands so as

not to burden others.

So does the Bible

ask us to act,

or to trust God?

Both at once,

and the text shows

it in scenes.

First example:

the Red Sea.

The Hebrews are trapped

between Pharaoh's army and

the sea.

Moses tells them,

in Exodus,

chapter fourteen:

still." But a few

verses later,

God says to Moses:

God acts,

and the people must

walk.

It is not "help

yourself first":

it is a help

that asks for a

response.

Second example:

Nehemiah.

Threatened by enemies while

rebuilding the walls of

Jerusalem,

he and his people

pray,

and they post guards

day and night.

Prayer and vigilance,

together.

Third example,

in Proverbs:

Yahweh." We prepare the

horse.

We prepare the army.

But we know where

victory lies.

And another proverb says:

steps." You plan,

you move forward,

and you admit that

you do not control

everything.

Psalm one hundred twenty-seven

says it even more

strongly:

who build it." The

builders really do build.

But their effort is

not enough.

That is the true

balance:

the biblical text says

neither "act alone" nor

"do nothing." It says:

act,

and do not put

your trust in your

own action alone.

So is the sentence

"God helps those who

help themselves" false?

Not entirely.

It contains a measure

of practical wisdom.

But it becomes dangerous

when we turn it

into a law of

God.

First,

it suggests that God's

help must be earned.

Yet the whole Bible

speaks of grace.

Second,

it can hurt those

who suffer.

If you are sick,

in debt,

exhausted or out of

work,

the sentence can sound

like a reproach:

"If God doesn't help

you,

it's because you aren't

helping yourself enough." The

book of Job says

exactly the opposite.

Job's friends explain that

his misfortune must come

from his own fault.

In the end,

God proves them wrong:

has." Finally,

the Bible does not

leave the poor to

fend for themselves.

The law of Moses

asks that gleanings be

left in the fields,

so that the poor

can eat.

And the book of

Proverbs says:

reward him." Here,

the Bible does not

tell the poor "help

yourself." It tells the

others:

help them.

If you are going

through a very heavy

time,

of exhaustion,

discouragement or depression,

these verses do not

ask you to manage

alone.

Speaking to a doctor,

a psychologist or someone

close to you can

help,

and faith does not

stand against care.

So what do we

do with this sentence?

You can keep it

as a proverb of

common sense:

act,

get ready,

don't stand with folded

arms.

But do not make

it a promise of

God.

God's promise is the

one in the Psalms:

Yahweh." One question,

if you want to

answer:

in what moment of

your life did you

feel that help came

to you without your

doing anything to deserve

it?

Tell us in the

comments.

Let's go back to

the carter stuck in

the mud.

Hercules tells him to

push the wheel.

The Bible says something

like this:

push the wheel,

and call out too.

Both are allowed:

doing what is within

your reach,

and entrusting the rest

to the One whose

help never fails.
